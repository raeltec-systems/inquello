# Deep-research prompt: how internal auditors actually do their work

> Run this in your deep-research tool **with the attached PDF** *Global Internal Audit Standards* (IIA, published 9 January 2024, effective 9 January 2025). Paste everything below the line.

---

## Role and purpose

You are a senior internal-audit methodologist and researcher. I am designing software that helps internal auditors carry out sound internal audit work. Before designing anything, I need a **sourced, practitioner-accurate map of how internal audit work is actually done**: what any internal auditor must understand, decide, do and communicate at each stage, and which parts legitimately vary between organisations. Optimise the report for accuracy, traceability and practical detail, not persuasion.

The attached *Global Internal Audit Standards* (the "Standards") is the **primary source**. Cite it by **Domain, Principle, Standard number and page** for every claim drawn from it.

## How to treat my framing

My working hypotheses are below. **They are things to test, not facts.** Confirm, refine or contradict each one with evidence, and say plainly where the evidence disagrees.

- **H1. Judgment chain.** For an assurance engagement, the auditor builds a judgment another person must be able to understand and defend. It develops through this chain: business objective → risks → controls and criteria → testing → evidence → findings and conclusion → management action → follow-up. (This is my summary, not an IIA diagram.)
- **H2. Iteration.** The work is iterative, not linear. Discoveries during testing can change the understanding of risk, send the auditor back to management, or require a different test. I believe the Standards make this point around page 92. Verify it, quote it, and find any other passages on iteration.
- **H3. Six stages, with cross-cutting themes:**
  1. deciding what to audit (the risk-based internal audit plan, before any engagement);
  2. understanding and planning an engagement;
  3. designing the work;
  4. performing the work;
  5. forming and discussing findings;
  6. reporting and follow-up.

  **Supervision, professional judgment, documentation, communication and independence or objectivity** run through all six; they are not end-of-process steps.
- **H4. Three layers:**
  - a **shared professional foundation** of principles, responsibilities and outcomes common to all internal audit;
  - a **configurable methodology**: each team's templates, terminology, rating scales, review stages, report formats and approval arrangements;
  - an **engagement context**: the specific business, risks, systems, evidence and people.

  A bank and a manufacturer may document audits differently, yet both must meet the foundation.

## Classification required on every claim

Tag each claim with exactly one of these labels:

- **[REQ]** A mandatory element of the Standards: the "Requirements" sections, plus the Purpose, Ethics and Professionalism and other mandatory text as the Standards define it. Quote the operative wording ("must").
- **[GUIDE]** Non-mandatory IIA material: "Considerations for Implementation", "Examples of Evidence of Conformance", Topical Requirements guidance, and IIA Global Guidance such as Practice Guides and GTAGs.
- **[TOPICAL]** An IIA Topical Requirement, for example Cybersecurity. State its status and effective date.
- **[LOCAL]** A legal, regulatory or sector requirement outside the IIA, for example banking supervisors or public-sector rules. Name the jurisdiction.
- **[PRACTICE]** Observed practice: firm methodologies, audit-software workflows, practitioner literature, or job descriptions.

If the Standards explain how mandatory and non-mandatory material interact, for example how "must" and "should" are used, summarise that first and cite it.

## Research questions

### Part A: the six stages

For **each** stage in H3, answer these questions:

1. **Purpose and outcome.** What this stage must achieve, and how you know it is done.
2. **Understand.** What the auditor must learn, and from whom or where.
3. **Decide.** The key judgments made here, the criteria for making them, and who has authority to make them: the auditor, the engagement supervisor, the CAE, the board, or management.
4. **Do.** The concrete activities, in realistic order. Mark where loops back to earlier stages happen, and what triggers them.
5. **Communicate.** With whom, about what, when, and in what form.
6. **Inputs and outputs.** The artefacts consumed and produced, for example the risk universe, audit plan, engagement planning memo, risk and control matrix, work programme, information request list, sampling plan, workpapers, issue or observation records, draft report, management action plans, and follow-up evidence. For each artefact, give its typical contents and who reviews or approves it.
7. **Evidence and documentation.** What must be documented, the standard for "sufficient, reliable, relevant and useful" information, and how review and sign-off work.
8. **What varies.** Which elements are foundation-fixed [REQ]; which are methodology choices that typically vary, with concrete examples; and which depend on engagement context.
9. **Common failure modes.** Typical quality-assessment findings or deficiencies at this stage, and the reasons they occur.

Stage-specific depth I need:

- **Stage 1:**
  - How the audit universe and risk assessment are built and refreshed.
  - Use of the organisation's strategy and its enterprise risk management.
  - Coordination and reliance on other assurance providers (the three lines model).
  - Board approval and changes to the plan.
- **Stage 2:**
  - Understanding the activity: process walkthroughs, interviews and data.
  - The engagement-level risk and control assessment.
  - Agreeing objectives, scope and limitations.
  - Engagement communication with management.
  - What "criteria" means, and what happens when criteria are absent.
- **Stage 3:**
  - Defining what "good" looks like.
  - Choosing between test types: inquiry, observation, inspection, reperformance and analytics.
  - Sample size and sampling method.
  - Information requests.
  - Staffing and competence.
  - The work programme, and its approval.
- **Stage 4:**
  - Obtaining and evaluating evidence, including judging its reliability.
  - Use of data analytics.
  - Investigating exceptions.
  - When to expand testing or change approach.
  - Handling suspected fraud.
  - Supervision during fieldwork.
- **Stage 5:**
  - Finding attributes: the "5 Cs" (criteria, condition, cause, effect or consequence, and corrective action or recommendation), or whatever the Standards actually require. Show exactly what is required and what is only common practice.
  - Root-cause analysis.
  - Rating or prioritising findings.
  - Validating facts with management.
  - Agreeing action plans, including owners and dates.
  - Handling disagreement and risk acceptance.
- **Stage 6:**
  - Engagement conclusions or opinions.
  - Report content requirements.
  - Distribution.
  - Errors and omissions in final communications.
  - Monitoring and validating management actions.
  - Escalating unresolved risk acceptance to the board.
  - Any overall or thematic opinions.

### Part B: the cross-cutting themes

For **supervision, professional judgment and skepticism, documentation, communication, and independence and objectivity**, describe:
- how each shows up at every stage;
- what is mandatory;
- what typically varies;
- what evidence of it a quality reviewer expects to see.

### Part C: advisory versus assurance

How do advisory (consulting) engagements differ in each stage? Cover agreement with the client, scope, documentation and communication.

### Part D: industry test cases

Test H3 and H4 against at least four contrasting settings:
1. **A bank or regulated financial institution.** Cover supervisory expectations of internal audit, for example the Basel Committee's principles for the internal audit function in banks, and current national supervisor rules where relevant.
2. **A manufacturer.**
3. **A public-sector body.**
4. **One more** of your choice, such as healthcare, a non-profit or a technology company.

For each setting:
- what stays the same;
- what changes in the stages, artefacts, terminology, ratings, approvals or reporting;
- any [LOCAL] requirements that add to or modify the Standards.

### Part E: vocabulary

Build a **glossary** of 40–60 terms practitioners use across the stages. For each term give:
- a one-line definition;
- the Standards' term, if it differs;
- common synonyms used by different methodologies, for example observation, finding or issue; or work programme versus audit program; or RCM versus risk and control matrix.

## Source policy

- **Primary:** the attached Standards. Then other IIA sources: Topical Requirements, Global Guidance, Practice Guides, GTAGs, and the IIA's own explanatory material on the 2024 Standards.
- **For [LOCAL]:** go directly to regulator and standard-setter sites. Examples are the Basel Committee (BIS), national banking supervisors, and government audit bodies. Verify that each regulatory claim is **currently in force** and give its date.
- **For [PRACTICE]:** published methodologies of large audit firms and professional bodies; documentation of widely used audit-management software showing actual workflows (for example workpaper, review and issue-tracking flows); quality-assessment and peer-review findings; credible practitioner literature; and job descriptions that name real tasks.
- **Freshness:** use 2024 or later for anything describing the Standards. Material written for the superseded 2017 IPPF is acceptable only when you mark it "pre-2024 IPPF" and note what changed. Practice and software claims: within the last 3 years. Regulatory status: verify it is current, whatever its date.
- **Two independent sources** are required for:
  - every [LOCAL] regulatory claim;
  - every claim that something is "common practice";
  - any statistic, for example adoption of analytics or typical sample sizes.

  If only one source exists, say so.
- Do **not** cite AI-generated content farms, undated blog posts, or vendor marketing pages that have no workflow detail.
