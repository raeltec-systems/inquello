# Standards structure; mandatory vs guidance  
The 2024 Standards are organized into five Domains (e.g. I. Purpose of Internal Auditing; V. Performing Internal Audit Services), 15 Principles, and 52 Standards.  Each Standard has two parts: **Requirements** (mandatory, denoted by “*must*”) and **Considerations for Implementation** (non-mandatory guidance, denoted “*should*” or “*may*”).  IIA guidance and examples are explicitly labeled “Considerations” or “Examples of Evidence” and are advisory.  The Standards state that inability to meet a *must* requires documenting the rationale and how the intent is still met.  In summary, “must” = [REQ] (mandatory) and “should/may” = [GUIDE] (preferred practice).  

# Verdicts on H1–H4  
- **H1 (Judgment chain)**: *Confirmed in broad form, with refinements.* The Standards emphasize risk‑based judgment from objectives through findings to follow-up.  They require linking engagement objectives to organizational objectives, establishing criteria, testing controls, and developing findings/conclusions.  Findings are defined as gaps between criteria and condition.  They require communicating findings (with causes/effects and recommended actions) to management and board.  Follow-up (“confirm implementation”) is explicitly required.  Thus, the conceptual chain (objective→risks→controls→testing→evidence→findings/conclusion→action→follow-up) is largely supported by the Standards, though the terminology and order may vary.  For example, Standards speak of *engagement objectives* (vs “business objective”), *evaluation criteria*, etc..  Notably, the Standards do not require using the “five Cs” label (criteria, condition, cause, consequence, corrective action) explicitly, but do require stating criteria, condition (gap), root cause and effect, and recommendations.  We classify H1 as **confirmed with refinement**, since Standards use slightly different terms but the underlying logic holds.  

- **H2 (Iteration)**: *Confirmed.* Domain V explicitly says engagement steps “are not always distinct, linear and sequential” and may overlap or loop (e.g. planning and risk assessment continue throughout the engagement).  The Standards give several examples of iterative work: if initial analyses yield insufficient evidence, auditors “must exercise professional care to determine whether additional analyses are required” and adjust the work program as needed.  If auditors and management disagree on findings/recommendations, the methodology may involve further discussion or even attaching statements.  Thus the Standards explicitly embed iteration and feedback loops (e.g. revisit risk assessment or scope when evidence changes).  This strongly **confirms H2**, including the cited p.92 reference (Domain V intro). 

- **H3 (Six stages + cross‑cutting)**: *Refined.* The six stages (planning the plan; engagement planning; designing tests; fieldwork; findings; reporting/follow-up) are a useful breakdown.  The Standards group some elements differently (e.g. “audit plan” is in Domain IV: Managing IAF) but they support these stages.  For example, Standard 9.4 mandates an annual risk-based **audit plan** (Stage 1); Standards 13.1–13.4 cover **planning an engagement** (Stage 2–3); Standards 14.1–14.3 cover **performing fieldwork** and developing findings (Stages 4–5); Standards 14.4–15.1 cover **forming recommendations and final reporting** (Stage 5–6); and Standard 15.2 covers **follow-up** (Stage 6).  Supervision, judgment, documentation, communication, and independence are indeed required throughout (e.g. supervisory review of workpapers, professional skepticism, documentation sufficiency, clear communication, organizational independence).  Thus H3’s stage list broadly holds, though terminology and grouping differ slightly.  We mark H3 as **refined** (stages are valid, but drawn partly from guidance rather than explicit domain separation).  

- **H4 (Three layers)**: *Confirmed.* The Standards themselves embody a **foundational layer** (the Principles and mandatory standards apply to all auditors); methodologies can vary in detail (so a firm’s templates or techniques is a separate layer); and the engagement context (specific risks/controls) is third.  For example, all IAFs must have a charter and plan (foundation), but firms choose how to document workpapers (methodology), and audit content depends on the particular organization (context).  H4 is **confirmed**: the Standards impose a common “foundation” (Domain I–V), while acknowledging that detailed formats, rating scales, etc. are part of each IAF’s methodology. 

# Part A: Stage-by-stage findings  

### Stage 1: Audit universe and plan ([Domain III–IV])  
- **Purpose/outcome**: Establish a risk-based **audit plan** covering all significant processes, aligned with strategy and objectives.  Done when a documented, board-approved plan exists. [REQ]  
- **Understand**: Learn the organization’s strategy, objectives and risk framework (e.g. ERM/risk register).  Input comes from board and management strategy docs, risk registers, prior audits, interviews, industry analyses. [PRACTICE]  
- **Decide**: Judgments on audit universe scope and priorities.  Criteria: organizational objectives, risk assessments (likelihood/impact) and risk appetite.  CAE normally sets methodology; board provides oversight of plan approval. [REQ]  
- **Do**: Build/update the *audit universe*: list all auditable entities (e.g. processes, locations) tailored to the business.  Perform risk assessment for each area (financial, operational, compliance, fraud, IT etc.).  Rank areas by significance and map to organizational objectives.  Prepare annual internal audit plan, and submit to senior management/board for review and approval (board approves the final plan).  Monitor for changes (e.g. new risks or projects) and update plan as needed.  Common loop: if new risks emerge mid-year, revise the universe/plan and get re-approval. [REQ]  
- **Communicate**: Draft plan is communicated to and approved by the board/audit committee.  The CAE also reviews plan assumptions with management.  Ongoing: significant plan changes are promptly communicated to the board. [REQ]  
- **Inputs/outputs**: Inputs include strategic plans, risk registers, previous audits, regulatory requirements.  Outputs: the *Internal Audit Charter* (governing mandate); *audit universe* list; annual risk assessment report; the *Internal Audit Plan* (objectives, scope, resources, schedule).  The board reviews and approves the Plan. [REQ]  
- **Documentation/evidence**: Document the universe and risk assessment (e.g. risk heat map, control matrices) and the approved plan.  Evidence of sufficiency: see Standard 14.6 – docs must allow replication.  Supervisor/CAE sign-off on the plan is required. [REQ]  
- **What varies**: The *foundation* requires a board-approved risk-based plan.  *Methodology* choices vary: e.g. how risks are scored (numeric vs qualitative scales), the frequency of plan refresh (annually vs mid-year updates), and templates used.  *Contextual* factors: industry (banks might emphasize capital/credit risks, manufacturers on production/quality).  [REQ/GUIDE/PRACTICE]  
- **Common failures**: Risk assessment not updated, plan not clearly linked to objectives, lacking board approval or documentation.  Often QA reports find missing risk rationale or no evidence of periodic plan update.  Other failures: not coordinating with external audit/other assurance (3LoD), or ignoring resource constraints. [PRACTICE]

### Stage 2: Engagement understanding and planning (Domain V – 13.x)  
- **Purpose/outcome**: Define *engagement objectives, scope, criteria,* and preliminarily assess risks/controls for the specific audit.  Success: a signed **engagement planning memo** (or documented plan) with objectives, scope, limitations, criteria, resource estimates and approved work program. [GUIDE]  
- **Understand**: Learn the client activity: process walkthroughs, interviews with management/staff, review policies, org charts, system documentation, previous audit findings, and data (reports, metrics, risk documents).  The IAF also considers entity-level risks (fraud, IT) during planning. [PRACTICE]  
- **Decide**: Set *engagement objectives and scope*. Criteria for these decisions: materiality and risk significance, board/senior management requests, and resources. Authority typically lies with the CAE or engagement manager (some methods require CAE sign-off).  Agree with management on deliverables (audit charter or engagement letter may formalize this).  Select evaluation criteria (policies, best practices) against which controls will be assessed.  If no criteria exist, Standard 13.4 requires auditors to obtain/agree on them with stakeholders. [REQ]  
- **Do**: Preliminary engagement risk assessment: identify and prioritize risks of the area under review.  Determine control environment and significant processes.  Draft objectives, scope (inclusion/exclusion) and limitations; determine key criteria (e.g. compliance standards, KPIs).  Plan meetings with client to clarify expectations; possibly conduct a *kick-off meeting*.  Prepare draft engagement planning memo or planning workpaper. [PRACTICE]  
- **Communicate**: Notify management of the engagement objectives, scope, and timing (per Standard 13.1).  This may be via a planning letter or meeting.  Document any scope limitations (management-imposed or practical) and communicate these to the board if needed. [REQ]  
- **Inputs/outputs**: Inputs: audit plan (area to audit), risk universe, prior audit reports, process documentation, regulatory requirements.  Outputs: engagement planning memo, risk/control matrix, documentation of criteria, scope and resources, and approved work program.  The engagement supervisor/CAE typically reviews and approves the planning memo/work program. [GUIDE]  
- **Documentation/evidence**: Document the basis for objectives and scope in workpapers; record identified criteria and risks.  The planning memo/work program and risk assessment should be approved and signed by the engagement supervisor/CAE.  Planning documentation must be sufficient for another auditor to understand reasoning (Standard 14.6). [REQ]  
- **What varies**: *Foundation*: IIA requires objectives and scope be documented and agreed.  *Methodology choices*: Some IAFs use formal engagement letters; some use planning checklists.  Level of formality (detailed vs simple memo), and exact criteria used (company policies vs external benchmarks) can vary.  *Context*: For a compliance audit vs financial audit vs operational audit, the emphasis on documentation and specific inputs will differ.  [GUIDE/PRACTICE]  
- **Common failures**: Vague objectives or scope; missing criteria (leads to weak findings); no documentation of interviews/walkthroughs.  Auditors sometimes forget to re-communicate scope changes (Standard 13.1).  Other issues: not involving management early, or ignoring board-agreed focus (if escalation needed). [PRACTICE]

### Stage 3: Designing audit procedures (Domain V – 13.x, 14.x)  
- **Purpose/outcome**: Define what "good" (the criteria) and the audit procedures to test controls—i.e. finalize the *work program*. Outcome: a detailed work program, including test objectives and steps for each control/risk, sampling plans, and request lists. [REQ]  
- **Understand**: Based on planning, confirm key controls and desired outcomes. Identify relevant frameworks or policies that define the “good” state (criteria).  If organizational criteria are inadequate, engage board/senior mgmt to establish criteria.  Assess skill needs and any needed technology. [REQ]  
- **Decide**: Select test types: *inspection*, *observation*, *inquiry*, *reperformance*, *analysis*.  Criteria: nature of control, cost/efficiency, and reliability needed.  Choose sampling strategy (statistical vs judgmental) based on risk and population (higher risk → larger, more formal sample).  Assign qualified staff (match skills to tests).  Approve budget/time (CAE or audit manager). [PRACTICE]  
- **Do**: Write detailed audit steps in the work program (per Standard 13.1).  Collect data requests (documents, reports, system extracts).  Plan any IT/data analytics tests; identify sources/databases.  Submit requests to client and obtain needed data access.  Prepare sampling plan: e.g. random vs haphazard; statistical vs non-statistical.  Prepare and get supervisor’s approval of the work program. [PRACTICE]  
- **Communicate**: Keep management informed of planned procedures in case of concerns (e.g. if access issues).  Internal: work program is a control document, not typically shared with auditee.  Confirm scope changes with management as needed (standard 13.1).  [GUIDE]  
- **Inputs/outputs**: Inputs: process maps, control documentation from Stage 2; audit manuals/templates.  Outputs: the *engagement work program* with step-by-step tests (with fields for completion dates and audit personnel); sampling plan; resource/training plans.  The supervisor/CAE reviews and signs off on the work program. [REQ]  
- **Documentation/evidence**: Work program must be comprehensive and approved.  Auditors document sampling rationale and method in the workpapers (defining population, sample, etc.).  Document criteria (“what is expected”) alongside each step. [REQ]  
- **What varies**: *Foundation*: CAE must ensure there is a methodology and that work is performed to it.  *Methodology*: Methods differ – e.g. small audit might skip sampling and test 100% (census), or rely on analytical review versus detailed testing.  Use of data analytics is encouraged when practical (some IAFs excel in analytics, others not).  *Context*: Highly regulated fields (e.g. SOX audits) may require strict statistical sampling; smaller entities may rely on judgment.  Criteria (e.g. “materiality” or “significance”) are often set by methodology (some use numeric thresholds, others risk-matrix).  [REQ/GUIDE/PRACTICE]  
- **Common failures**: Inadequate test coverage, reliance on inquiry only, too small samples, or ignoring control weaknesses found.  QA reviews often cite missing documentation of sampling method or lack of evidence that data analytics were used when available.  Some teams neglect to revisit the work program when new risks surface (should loop back to planning). [PRACTICE]

### Stage 4: Fieldwork (Domain V – 14.1–14.3)  
- **Purpose/outcome**: Obtain and evaluate evidence on controls/transactions to identify any *conditions* (actual state) versus *criteria* (expected state).  Outcome: documented test results, evidence of performance or failure of controls. [REQ]  
- **Understand**: Continuously update knowledge from planning. Gather data (records, reports, system queries).  Use professional skepticism to assess reliability of information.  If something looks inconsistent or suspicious (e.g. unexpected results), investigate further. [REQ]  
- **Decide**: Determine when findings exist: per Standard 14.2, any difference between criteria and condition is a potential finding.  If initial tests reveal exceptions or anomalies, auditors must use judgment to evaluate risk and decide if more evidence is needed.  If significant new risk emerges, they may expand scope or amend the work program (subject to CAE approval).  [REQ]  
- **Do**: Execute audit steps: conduct control tests (walk-throughs, reperformance), inspect documents, observe processes, and perform inquiries.  Perform data analytics: use software to test full populations or focus samples.  Test samples and record exceptions.  When exceptions found, trace to root cause.  If evidence is insufficient or not reliable (e.g. source is internal and unverified), seek corroboration or alternate evidence.  Document all work in *workpapers*. [REQ/PRACTICE]  
- **Communicate**: Keep management informed of interim results (as needed).  Highlight urgent issues immediately (if fraud or serious risk discovered, inform audit committee).  Use progress meetings to catch misunderstandings.  Intra-team: question and discuss with supervisor if any conflict or ambiguity.  [GUIDE]  
- **Inputs/outputs**: Inputs: engagement work program, data/files obtained, entity policies.  Outputs: filled-out workpapers showing evidence and analysis, notes on test results, lists of exceptions.  These feed into findings.  [PRACTICE]  
- **Documentation/evidence**: Internal auditors *must gather* information that is relevant, reliable, and sufficient.  “Reliable” means factual, current, from independent/corroborated sources.  Evidence must be documented so another auditor could replicate the work.  Auditors must record why they chose their sample and evidence (see Standard 14.1 Considerations: sampling guidance).  Workpapers must be reviewed for accuracy/completeness by the engagement supervisor, and ultimately approved by the CAE. [REQ]  
- **What varies**: *Foundation*: auditors always need evidence per 14.1.  *Methodology*: Some IAFs heavily use data analytics and full population tests, others rely on sample-based manual tests.  Sampling method varies (random, stratified, judgmental).  *Context*: In highly computerized environments, testing might be automated and continuous; in smaller settings, auditors may do more walkthroughs and inquiries.  [GUIDE/PRACTICE]  
- **Common failures**: Collecting insufficient evidence or relying on unverified info (violates 14.1).  QA often notes missing back-up documentation or unclear cross-references.  Overlooking exceptions or not following up anomalies; not expanding sample when error rates appear high.  Failing to document reliability judgments or to involve supervisory review (14.6) is also a common deficiency. [PRACTICE]

### Stage 5: Findings, conclusions and action plans (Domain V – 14.3, 14.4, 15.1)  
- **Purpose/outcome**: Develop and agree final **findings** (gaps between criteria and condition, including root cause and impact), prioritize them, and obtain management’s corrective action plans.  Outcome: draft audit report (or closing memo) with agreed findings, recommendations and an overall conclusion. [REQ]  
- **Understand**: For each potential finding (difference noted in fieldwork), gather all relevant evidence and context. Work with staff or review documentation to determine *root cause* (discuss with process owner) and potential *effects/consequences*.  [REQ]  
- **Decide**: Determine finding attributes: every finding must be evaluated for significance (Standard 14.3).  Use likelihood and impact on objectives/governance as criteria.  Assign a priority or rating (if IAF methodology provides scales).  Decide if management’s explanation or action is adequate. [REQ]  
- **Do**: Draft each finding in a structured way: state the *criteria*, the *condition* (what was), the *cause* (why it happened), and *effect* (impact or magnitude).  Develop a recommendation that addresses the root cause.  Discuss each draft finding and recommended action with management to validate facts and obtain their draft action plan (owners and due dates).  Document agreed corrective actions; if management disagrees, follow methodology to resolve or document the impasse.  [REQ]  
- **Communicate**: Present draft findings to management and discuss prioritization/action.  After agreement, prepare the final engagement communication (report).  Ensure report narrative is accurate, objective, clear, concise and complete.  CAE must review and approve the final communication.  Distribute the report to those who can act (typically board/audit committee and senior management).  [REQ]  
- **Inputs/outputs**: Inputs: workpapers, analyses and evidence from fieldwork; draft finding write-ups.  Outputs: *final report* including objective/scope (15.1), findings with significance, conclusions, recommendations and management action plans.  Also produce issue logs or tracking spreadsheets with owners and due dates.  These must be reviewed by CAE before issuance. [REQ]  
- **Documentation/evidence**: All findings and conclusions must be substantiated by documented evidence.  The final communication *must* include findings and significance for assurance engagements and specify responsible parties and due dates for actions.  Findings must be traceable to the workpapers (see 14.6: docs cross-referenced).  Nonconformance with Standards (if any) must be disclosed in the final report.  [REQ]  
- **What varies**: *Foundation*: Findings must have criteria, condition, cause, effect and recommendations.  *Methodology*: Different IAFs use different terms (“observation” vs “finding”), rating labels (e.g. High/Medium/Low vs numbers), and report formats (memorandum vs formal report).  *Context*: In public sector, reports might use governmental terminology (e.g. “observation” under GAO); a manufacturing IAF might emphasize process safety issues, while an IT audit uses different criterion.  [GUIDE/PRACTICE]  
- **Common failures**: Reports missing root cause or impact analysis; recommendations that don’t address causes.  Prior QA findings: audit reports issued without specifying due dates or owners for actions (violates 15.1).  Also, not ranking findings by significance (though recommended) or poorly substantiating conclusions.  Disagreements not documented (Standard 14.4 says use methodology for resolution).  [PRACTICE]

### Stage 6: Reporting and follow-up (Domain V – 15.1–15.2)  
- **Purpose/outcome**: Deliver the final engagement results and ensure management acts on them. Outcome: a concluded engagement with a formal report (or final memo) and a closed loop on actions (evidence of follow-up). [REQ]  
- **Understand**: No new audit work – this stage synthesizes everything.  For assurance engagements, auditors form an overall **conclusion/opinion** on whether controls effectively managed risks.  For consulting engagements, conclusions are framed around objectives. [REQ]  
- **Decide**: Determine the final conclusion. As per Standard 14.5, this summarizes results vs objectives and overall significance.  For assurance, explicitly state judgment on effectiveness (or add a rating).  CAE (or delegate) decides if the audit “conforms with standards” (must be supported by QA results).  [REQ]  
- **Do**: Issue the final communication (report). Ensure it’s aligned with required content (see Stage 5).  The CAE signs or authorizes distribution of the report.  If any standards were not followed, the report must *disclose* this per 15.1.  After issuance, auditors maintain an action-tracking system. When feasible, schedule follow-up audits or procedures. [REQ]  
- **Communicate**: Distribute the report to all key stakeholders (board/audit committee, senior management, process owners).  Communicate if any serious issues remain unresolved (e.g. if management declined an action, escalate to board).  For high-priority findings, consider presenting in person.  [REQ]  
- **Inputs/outputs**: Inputs: finalized findings, recommendations, and action plans.  Outputs: the final audit report and audit evidence that actions have been implemented.  Follow-up outputs include meeting minutes or workpapers showing “closed” status of actions.  [REQ]  
- **Documentation/evidence**: The final communication **must** meet Standard 11.2 qualities (accurate, clear, objective, etc.).  It must include required elements (findings, conclusions, etc.).  Evidence of follow-up: Internal auditors must *confirm* implementation of actions using a risk‑based methodology.  This involves inquiries, re-testing, and tracking progress.  All follow-up results are documented (e.g. in a tracking log or follow-up audit report). If actions are overdue, auditors must report this and obtain explanation. [REQ]  
- **What varies**: *Foundation*: The need to report findings and follow-up is mandatory.  *Methodology*: Format of the final report varies by organization (formal report vs memo); tracking systems range from spreadsheets to audit software.  Not all IAFs issue a formal “opinion”; some give narrative conclusions or graded opinions.  *Context*: Some industries may expect specific reporting formats (e.g. public sector agencies use INTOSAI report templates).  [GUIDE/PRACTICE]  
- **Common failures**: Final report issued without all required sections (e.g. missing significance or conclusions).  Sometimes CAE approval is missed (noncompliance with 15.1).  Follow-up is often weak: IAFs may not perform actual re-tests or may never revisit old findings.  QA reports commonly note missing documentation of follow-up work (Standard 15.2). [PRACTICE]

# Part B: Cross-cutting themes  

- **Supervision**: At every stage, audit work is reviewed by a supervisor or manager.  The Standards require supervisors to review planning, workpapers and communications.  Supervisor and CAE sign-off is mandatory on final docs.  A quality reviewer expects to see review notes or sign-offs on planning memos, working papers and the draft report.  *Mandatory*: Standard 14.6 mandates reviewer and CAE approval of documentation.  *Varies*: The depth of review (e.g. senior audit manager vs CAE) varies by firm; some use peer review or audit committee oversight. [REQ]  

- **Professional judgment & skepticism**: Required at all stages.  Standard 4.3 explicitly requires “professional skepticism” throughout planning and execution.  Auditors must question contrary evidence and seek corroboration.  Judgment is needed in risk ranking (Stage 1), scoping (Stage 2), sampling (Stage 3), and when interpreting exceptions (Stage 4–5).  Evidence of skepticism includes documented follow-up on anomalies and questions noted in workpapers.  *Mandatory*: exercise of skepticism (14.1, 4.3).  *Varies*: Individual skill level, industry knowledge.  Reviewers look for documented use of judgment (e.g. footnotes on why a finding was significant or why a small sample was sufficient). [REQ/GUIDE]  

- **Documentation**: Must be thorough and timely.  All stages generate workpapers or memos that support later stages.  Standard 14.6 requires documentation be sufficient for repeat work.  *Mandatory*: capturing objective, scope, tests, results, conclusions.  *Varies*: Format (paper, software) and detail (narrative vs checklist) differ.  Auditors should mark sources, dates, and preparers.  QA expects evidence of planning, evidence gathering, and conclusion steps: e.g. risk assessment notes, sampling calculations, draft findings, supervisory notes. [REQ/PRACTICE]  

- **Communication**: Ongoing and at key milestones.  Standards require communication of objectives/scope at start (13.1) and final results at end (15.1).  Use mix of formal (reports, presentations) and informal (emails, meetings) methods.  *Mandatory*: final report qualities (clear, objective, etc.) and interim notices of changes or critical issues.  *Varies*: Some IAFs issue multiple status memos, others just final report.  Evidence of communication: copies of emails/plans, minutes of kickoff or closeout meetings, review of communications by quality reviewers. [REQ/GUIDE]  

- **Independence/Objectivity**: Must be maintained throughout.  The CAE must report functionally to the board to preserve independence.  Internal auditors must avoid conflicts of interest (Principle 2 on Objectivity).  *Mandatory*: CAE annual confirmation of independence (7.1).  *Varies*: In practice, audit functions may rotate staff through lines of business (but with safeguards).  QA expects evidence of independence: e.g. no auditor does a process they oversaw, disclosure of any role conflicts. [REQ]  

# Part C: Advisory vs Assurance engagements  

The Standards state that internal auditing includes both assurance and advisory services, and auditors must conform to the Standards in both modes (except where a Standard explicitly excludes advisory).  In **assurance** engagements, auditors provide an opinion or conclusion on controls/effectiveness; findings and recommendations lead to a conclusion about control effectiveness.  In **advisory** (consulting) engagements, the scope is defined by agreement with the client, and the deliverable is usually advice, recommendations or facilitation rather than a formal opinion.  The Standards note that for advisory engagements “gathering evidence to develop findings may not be necessary, depending on agreement with stakeholders”.  Communication requirements differ slightly: for advisory, the final “conclusion” should align with agreed objectives, and may not include a “control effectiveness” judgment.  Documentation is generally similar (planning, workpapers) but may be less formal; however, objectivity must still be maintained (auditors must not assume management roles).  In summary: assurance audits have defined objectives/scope per plan and end with an opinion on controls, whereas advisory projects are negotiated in scope, focused on advice, and apply standards only “as applicable” to the agreed deliverables.  [GUIDE/PRACTICE]

# Part D: Industry test cases  

**Bank / Regulated Financial**: Many elements are the same (risk-based planning, standards-based conduct).  However, banks have extra [LOCAL] requirements.  Basel and banking regulators emphasize internal audit independence and competence (e.g. Basel’s “Principle 9” – see Basel 2012 principles).  For example, regulators often *require* an audit charter, audit committee oversight, and coverage of credit, market, liquidity, operational risks.  A Saudi bank rulebook requires documenting all units/processes and risk ranking, with a 3–4 year audit cycle based on risk.  Banks typically use bank-specific risk language (e.g. loan quality, capital adequacy) and employ formal audit committees (instead of a generic “board”).  Internal audit plan is often reviewed by regulators too.  The Standards themselves apply unchanged; local rules may add e.g. specific scope (like periodic compliance audits under banking law) or reporting lines.  [LOCAL: SAMA, Basel]  

**Manufacturer (Commercial)**: The core audit process is the same (Stages 1–6 apply).  Differences: focus on operational efficiency, production quality, supply chain controls, inventory valuation.  Firms may use Lean or Six Sigma terminology.  Board involvement might be less formal (possibly no separate audit committee in a SME).  Templates (reports and checklists) vary by industry; some manufacturers emphasize continuous auditing of production data.  No major [LOCAL] regulatory mandates on internal audit (aside from SOX for public manufacturers).  Practice differences: might rely more on shop-floor walkthroughs, physical inventory observations, and less on financial transaction testing. [PRACTICE]  

**Public-Sector Body**: The stages still apply, but with different context.  An oversight body (e.g. audit committee of legislators) replaces the corporate board.  Scope often includes performance and compliance auditing (not just financial/operational).  Reporting may be to public accounts committees or audit boards.  The Standards align largely with INTOSAI standards (e.g. ISSAI 1300s) but with public-sector lingo.  [LOCAL: INTOSAI, GAO Yellow Book] For example, government audit charters and annual audit programs are often required by statute.  Documentation may require higher transparency (often public reports) and follow-up through official channels.  Terminology differs: “finding” vs “observation”; “recommendation” vs “corrective action plan”. [GUIDE/PRACTICE]  

**Healthcare (example)**: Remains risk-based but with healthcare specifics (clinical risk, patient safety).  Regulations like HIPAA (US) or medical audit codes (e.g. HTA in UK) add compliance requirements.  Boards often require internal audit to cover regulatory compliance (e.g. reporting of health data breaches).  Reporting might include separate sections on patient safety.  Audit software might use healthcare data feeds.  [LOCAL: Health Regulations]  

For each setting, the *foundation* (need for a plan, quality work, independence) stays constant, but *terminology, audit universe categories, and deliverables* vary.  For example, a bank’s audit report might rank issues by capital impact, whereas a public agency’s report might rate “priority and risk to service delivery.”  Approval processes differ (public-sector audits often require audit committee clearance of findings).  [LOCAL] requirements like Basel principles (financial) or GOV.UK’s “Minimum Standards” for local government audit add specificity to the Standards’ requirements.  

# Iteration map  

Identified loops (trigger → loop back):  
- **Finding insufficient evidence** (trigger: tests inconclusive) → return to *Design* stage to expand tests.  
- **New risk emerges during fieldwork** (trigger: new control gap found) → return to *Planning* stage to adjust scope/risk assessment. (Implied by iterative nature) [INFERENCE]  
- **Scope change requested by management** (trigger: client constraint discovered) → revisit *Stage 2* to re-agree scope (Standard 13.1 requires re-communicating changes).  
- **Disagreement on finding/recommendation** (trigger: management dispute) → engage CAE or board; may return to *Review/Finding development* stage (Standard 13.1,14.4).  
- **Management action taken before report** (trigger: mgmt starts implementing fix) → acknowledge in *Stage 6 report* (Standard 15.1).  

# Part E: Glossary (selected terms)  

- **Audit charter** (Standards term: *Internal Audit Charter*): Formal document (board-approved) that defines the internal audit function’s purpose, authority and responsibility. *Synonyms:* IAF charter, audit mandate. [REQ]  
- **Audit committee**: A board subcommittee overseeing internal audit, receiving reports, approving plan, and ensuring IA independence. *Synonyms:* committee of the board, oversight committee. (The Standards assume either an audit committee or equivalent governing body.) [PRACTICE]  
- **Audit universe**: Inventory of all auditable entities/processes. *Synonyms:* auditable areas, risk universe. (Regulatory term – e.g. SAMA requires listing all units.) [PRACTICE]  
- **Board**: The organization’s governing body (e.g. audit committee of board of directors; for government, may be council or ministerial committee). *Synonyms:* governing board, trustees. (Standard 6.3 calls for board support.) [REQ]  
- **Criteria**: Desired state or benchmark used to evaluate the activity. *Synonyms:* evaluation criteria, standards. If none exist, auditors must establish them with management. [REQ]  
- **Condition**: The actual state of the activity found (i.e. what exists). *Synonyms:* current state, “what is”. (Not defined in excerpt, but implies by “difference between criteria and condition”.) [INFERENCE]  
- **Control**: A policy or procedure that helps manage risk. *Synonyms:* control activity, safeguard. (The Standards assume use of existing controls for assurance testing.) [INFERENCE]  
- **Engagement**: A specific internal audit assignment or project with defined objectives. *Synonyms:* audit engagement, audit assignment, assignment. [REQ]  
- **Engagement objectives**: The specific goals of an engagement (derived from organizational objectives). *Synonyms:* audit objectives, scope objectives. [REQ]  
- **Engagement work program**: Document listing tasks and procedures to achieve engagement objectives. *Synonyms:* audit program, workplan. [REQ]  
- **Exception**: A deviation from expected results (i.e. a control failure or misstatement). *Synonyms:* audit exception, discrepancy. (Implied by “analyses to find exceptions” in 14.2.) [INFERENCE]  
- **Finding (observation)**: A determination that a gap exists between criteria and condition. *Synonyms:* observation, issue. [REQ]  
- **Implication/Consequence**: The effect or impact of a finding on the organization (not a formal term in Standards, but part of evaluating significance). *Synonyms:* impact, consequence, effect. [GUIDE]  
- **Management action plan (MAP)**: Agreed corrective actions with owners and target dates. *Synonyms:* corrective action plan, remediation plan. (The Standards use "action plans" interchangeably with recommendations.) [REQ]  
- **Overall conclusion (opinion)**: Summary judgment on the engagement results relative to objectives. *Synonyms:* audit opinion (though Standards avoid that word), final conclusion. [REQ]  
- **Plan (audit plan)**: The overall strategy and program of the internal audit function (strategic/annual plan). *Synonyms:* annual audit plan, strategic plan. [REQ]  
- **Professional skepticism**: An attitude of questioning and critically assessing information. *Synonyms:* critical mindset, professional doubt. [REQ]  
- **Professional judgment**: Application of relevant training and experience in making informed decisions during audit. *Synonyms:* auditor judgment, audit judgment. (Implied principle throughout IPPF.) [INFERENCE]  
- **Risk assessment**: Process of identifying and analyzing risks to objectives; basis for audit planning. *Synonyms:* risk evaluation, risk analysis. [REQ]  
- **Risk and Control Matrix (RCM)**: A tool listing risks, controls, and tests. *Synonyms:* audit matrix, risk matrix. (Common in methodology, not in Standards.) [PRACTICE]  
- **Sampling**: Reviewing a representative subset of a population. *Synonyms:* audit sampling. (Standards encourage representative sampling.) [PRACTICE]  
- **Scope**: Boundaries of the audit (what is and isn’t included). *Synonyms:* engagement scope. [REQ]  
- **Statement of Work (SOW)**: Document detailing agreed audit tasks (used in project/consulting context). *Synonyms:* engagement charter (less formal). [PRACTICE]  
- **Status report**: Update on engagement progress; may be informal or formal. *Synonyms:* interim report. [PRACTICE]  
- **Strategy (audit strategy)**: Long-term approach of the IA function (multi-year plan). *Synonyms:* strategic plan for IAF. [REQ]  
- **Walk-through**: Technique of tracing a transaction from start to finish. *Synonyms:* process walk, walkthrough. [PRACTICE]  
- **Workpaper**: Documentation of internal audit work; supports findings. *Synonyms:* audit worksheet, workpapers. [REQ]

# Contradictions and open questions  

- Some sources suggest fixed “five C” structure for findings, but the Standards do not explicitly use that format. We choose to frame findings by criteria/condition/cause/effect/action as guidance.  
- Limited references were found on “iteration” beyond Domain V introduction. We assume iterative practices (e.g. agile audits), but the Standards explicitly mention only the general overlapping nature of steps.  
- Banking regulations (Basel, etc.) were referenced via secondary sources; it’s not easy to cite text from Basel without official links. The SAMA rulebook was one concrete [LOCAL] example.  
- Public-sector differences are inferred from practice; direct ISSAI/Yellow Book citations were not found in the connected sources (beyond stating advisory vs assurance). The applicability of the IIA Standards to government audit is assumed by principle but not explicitly documented.  
- [INFERENCE] labels mark statements based on logical deduction from cited material (e.g. “continue risk assessment if new info appears”).
